School readiness is a multidimensional construct influenced by familial, socio-economic, behavioral, and institutional factors. While prior research has established correlational relationships between these factors and academic outcomes, causal evidence remains limited. This study applies modern causal inference methods to investigate the causal impact of family support, learning motivation, sleep routines, locus of control, and developmental factors on academic readiness, operationalized as students' average academic achievement (Av). Using observational data from a comprehensive school readiness assessment conducted in the 2018–2019 academic year (N = 148), we employ the Potential Outcomes framework, Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), Average Treatment Effect (ATE) estimation, Propensity Score Matching (PSM), Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW), and Doubly Robust Estimation to address confounding and selection bias. Results from descriptive analyses, independent-samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, Pearson correlations, and multiple OLS regression (R² = 0.548) reveal that motivation, sleep procedure, locus of control, socioeconomic status, developmental delay, and positive peer influence are robust predictors of academic readiness. Doubly Robust causal estimates demonstrate that observed group differences partially reflect pre-existing confounding, underscoring the importance of rigorous adjustment in educational research. These findings provide policy-relevant insights for early educational interventions.
